Research Lines

My work focuses on the synthesis and physical characterization of nanostructured transition-metal oxides, mainly manganese oxides, as nanoparticles and thin films. I want to understand how synthesis and deposition conditions control their structure, defects, and optical properties.

Nanomaterials

Size-dependent physics

Nanomaterials have at least one dimension between 1 and 100 nm. At this scale, two effects change their properties compared with the bulk material: the high fraction of surface atoms and quantum confinement.

  • Band gap widening as the crystallite size decreases
  • Surface defects and oxygen vacancies that control luminescence
  • High specific surface area for catalysis and energy storage
Focus: low-cost, scalable synthesis routes that tune size and defects without high-temperature treatments.

Manganese Oxide Mn3O4

Hausmannite · spinel

Mn3O4 (hausmannite) has a tetragonally distorted spinel structure (space group I41/amd). Mn2+ ions occupy tetrahedral sites and Mn3+ ions occupy octahedral sites. The Jahn–Teller distortion of Mn3+ is responsible for the tetragonal cell.

  • Stable, low-cost, and non-toxic
  • Bulk band gap ≈ 2.30 eV, tunable at the nanoscale
  • Applications: supercapacitors, Li-ion batteries, catalysis, sensors, and biomedicine
My result: Mn3O4 nanoparticles synthesized in one step at room temperature in 30 min, with a band gap of 2.37–2.89 eV. See publication →

Magnetron Sputtering

Thin-film deposition · PVD

Physical vapor deposition in which Ar+ ions from a plasma bombard a target and eject atoms that condense on a substrate. A magnetic field behind the target traps electrons near its surface, which increases ionization and the deposition rate.

  • DC mode for metallic targets; RF mode for insulating oxide targets
  • Reactive sputtering (Ar + O2) to grow oxides such as MnOx
  • Control parameters: power, working pressure, Ar/O2 ratio, substrate temperature, and time
  • Dense, uniform, adherent films with controlled thickness

Spin Coating

Solution-based films

A precursor solution or nanoparticle dispersion is deposited on a substrate, which is then spun at high speed. Centrifugal force spreads the liquid, and solvent evaporation leaves a uniform film.

  • Typical speeds: 1000–6000 rpm for 30–60 s
  • Thickness scales roughly as ω−1/2, and also depends on viscosity and concentration
  • Multilayers by repeated cycles, followed by drying and annealing
  • Simple, fast, and low-cost; ideal for depositing synthesized nanoparticles

Random Lasing

Lasers without a cavity

A conventional laser needs mirrors to form an optical cavity. In a random laser, the feedback comes instead from multiple scattering of light in a disordered medium with optical gain, for example a dye solution with dispersed nanoparticles. Light performs a random walk between the scatterers. When the gain along those paths exceeds the losses, laser emission appears.

  • Proposed theoretically by V. S. Letokhov (1968). N. M. Lawandy and co-workers reported it experimentally in 1994, using TiO2 nanoparticles in a laser dye.
  • Signatures: a pump threshold, strong spectral narrowing, and, with coherent feedback, sharp spectral spikes.
  • Nanoparticles of oxides such as TiO2 and ZnO act as efficient light scatterers.
  • Applications: speckle-free imaging, sensing, photonic barcodes, and low-cost, flexible light sources.
Link to my work: nanostructured oxides with controlled size and morphology are natural candidates as scattering media for random lasers.

Synthesis Route

One-step coprecipitation of Mn3O4 nanoparticles at room temperature, with no post-synthesis thermal treatment. Water or ethanol is used as the solvent to tune particle size and band gap.

STEP 1PrecursorsMnCl2·4H2O 0.1 M + NaOH 0.1–0.3 M
STEP 2MixingStirring at 20 °C in water or ethanol
STEP 3Aging30 min at 20 °C, forming a colloid
STEP 4Washing3 washes with double-distilled water
STEP 5Drying70 °C for 12 h, then agate mortar
RESULTMn3O4 NPsSpherical, 30–54 nm

Characterization Techniques

Structural, vibrational, optical, and morphological techniques that I use to connect synthesis conditions with the physical properties of nanomaterials.

XRD Structure

X-ray diffraction
What it measures
Diffraction of X-rays by crystal planes (Bragg's law, nλ = 2d sinθ).
What I obtain
Phase identification, lattice parameters, crystallite size (Scherrer equation), microstrain, and dislocation density.
In Mn3O4
Single tetragonal phase. Crystallite size is 21–25 nm in water and 9–11 nm in ethanol.

Raman Vibrations

Raman spectroscopy
What it measures
Inelastic scattering of laser light by lattice vibrations (phonons).
What I obtain
Phase fingerprint and purity, local disorder, and bonding. It distinguishes between manganese oxide phases.
In Mn3O4
Eg ≈ 318, T2g ≈ 372, and A1g ≈ 658 cm−1 (Mn–O in tetrahedral sites). Excitation at 532 nm.

PL Defects

Photoluminescence
What it measures
Light emitted by the material after optical excitation.
What I obtain
Radiative transitions, defect states, and oxygen vacancies inside the band gap.
In Mn3O4
Excitation at 380 nm. Bands at 460, 490, 519, 541, and 589 nm, linked to oxygen vacancies and Mn3+ d–d transitions.

UV-Vis Band gap

Diffuse reflectance spectroscopy
What it measures
Absorption and reflectance of light from the ultraviolet to the near-infrared.
What I obtain
Optical band gap from the Kubelka–Munk function and the Tauc plot.
In Mn3O4
Eg = 2.37–2.89 eV, above the bulk value (2.30 eV), consistent with quantum confinement.

SEM Morphology

Scanning electron microscopy
What it measures
Secondary and backscattered electrons produced by a focused electron beam scanned over the surface.
What I obtain
Morphology, particle size distribution, agglomeration, and film surface and thickness.
In Mn3O4
Spherical particles: 46–54 nm in water and 30–33 nm in ethanol.

FTIR Bonds

Infrared spectroscopy
What it measures
Absorption of infrared radiation by molecular and lattice vibrations.
What I obtain
Metal–oxygen bonds, surface groups, and residues from the synthesis.
In Mn3O4
Mn–O bands at ≈ 403, 476, and 593 cm−1, confirming the spinel structure.
